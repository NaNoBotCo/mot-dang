01 / The problem, in one shop

A crawl gives you 487 identical records

4877-Elevens in the two provinces
1that OpenStreetMap tagged with a cash machine
92that Mot Dang knows have one
231that Mot Dang knows are open all night

Same brand, same logo, same Wikidata id, 487 pins. Nobody standing on a street at eleven at night is choosing between them on brand. They want the one with the ATM, or the one that is actually open, or the one with the hot food counter.

The unit of data is the shop. The unit of the decision is what is inside it. That gap is where every map on earth stops, and it is not a crawling problem — it is a collection problem wearing a crawl's clothes.

02 / What we collect instead

The facts that decide, and the reason we believe each one

Three layers, and they are data rather than code — a shelf says what kind of place this is, a facet says whether that one is worth the walk, a tag says what it also is across every shelf.

Every fact wears its warrant. A dashed border means it was joined from a map point nearby. Solid means it was tagged. Solid and bold means a person stood there. A reader can see how far to trust a tag before they tap it.

Absence is never a claim. An untagged facet renders as nothing, never as "no". We know 92 of the 7-Elevens have a cash machine. We do not claim the other 395 lack one, and a directory that implied it would be lying quietly.

03 / One question, done properly

Where is the nearest toilet, may I use it, what does it cost

Sometimes there is only one question. The mapped answer is thin — 344 toilets mapped as toilets in Chiang Mai, 87 in Chiang Rai, and outside the old city that is close to nothing. Every map stops there and tells the reader there is nothing nearby.

So the answer is built a second way: thirteen classes of place that dependably keep one, ranked by how dependable the class is and how long its doors stay open — not by how nice the room is. Rank one is a fuel station. Each class carries who may use it (public, visitor, customer, guest), what it costs (free, small fee, free or small, with an order), and the hours its doors are open.

What is refused, and why

  • Convenience stores are excluded — all 515 of them. A Thai 7-Eleven does not normally keep a customer toilet, unlike abroad, and listing 515 of them would walk somebody to a door that is not there
  • The two kinds of knowledge — a toilet somebody mapped, and a class that dependably has one — are never printed in the same voice

What the reader gets

  • The list works the second the page opens, with no location shared and nothing to allow
  • Sorted by minutes on foot. Nearly every wat keeps one open and free
  • If a position is shared it stays on the device

This is the whole method in one page: when the ground truth is thin, answer with a ranked class, say which kind of knowing it is, and refuse the answer that would waste somebody's walk.

Built before anyone asked. A shop owner searching their own name finds a page with their hours, their pin, their phone — and a button that says this is mine. Confirming it takes a tick and shows on the page the same second.

That last number is the opportunity, not the problem. The supply side of a directory is normally the expensive half: you must persuade a merchant to create something. Here it exists already, and the ask is one tap on a page they were going to look at anyway.

The incumbent cannot run this play. A platform needs the merchant to show up first. Mot Dang did the work first and is holding the city's commercial identity in trust until its owners arrive.
