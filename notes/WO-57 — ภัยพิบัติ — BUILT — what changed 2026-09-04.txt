4 · SMOKE SEASON — /foon.html
------------------------------
    I went looking for the government clean-air rooms expecting to find
    nothing usable. กรมอนามัย keeps a national register with a public API,
    and it holds 2,428 PUBLICLY ACCESSIBLE rooms across our two provinces —
    every one with a phone, all 45 districts covered.

    A March 2026 newspaper piece said Chiang Mai had 45 public clean-air
    rooms in 13 of 25 districts. The register says 1,382 in all 26. The
    paper was counting one programme. This is why I read registers and not
    articles about registers.

    The grade rides on every row, because the register grades them: 221 were
    assessed by an officer, the rest assessed themselves, and some were last
    assessed in 2024. The page says which and when, and says ring first.

    THE PART THAT TOOK THE LONGEST, and the part I would want you to know:
    the register's pins are dirty. One row's latitude is 473027. Sixty rows
    are outside both provinces. Forty-five rows naming amphoe as far away as
    Mae Sai and Omkoi are pinned within five kilometres of Tha Phae Gate.
    The FIRST version of this page cheerfully offered a Mae Chaem health
    station as the nearest clean-air room, 226 metres away and 90 km wrong.

    So there are now two checks. Both were tuned against a set of rows I
    could prove were wrong, not by feel. Both FLAG rather than delete: a
    flagged row keeps its place and its phone and only loses its seat in the
    "nearest to me" list. 206 pins set aside, and the page says so.

    One phone number was on 428 rows at 246 separate sites — a bulk-entry
    default someone typed into a batch. 458 rows lost a number that was
    never theirs.

5 · THE RIVER — /nam.html — AND WHY IT IS NOT WHAT I OFFERED
-------------------------------------------------------------
    I proposed a flood layer built on the terrain data already cached here,
    and said in the same breath that it could not tell you whether your
    house floods. I did not build it. That was the right call and here is
    what replaced it.

    The Royal Irrigation Department publishes hourly readings for every
    gauge on the Ping — and every single row carries THAT STATION'S OWN
    ALERT LEVEL. P.1 at Nawarat Bridge states 3.70 m. So the page never has
    to model anything: it prints the reading, the RID's own limit beside it,
    which way the last six hours went, and the river drawn in the order the
    water passes — Chiang Dao, Cho Lae, Mae Tae, Nawarat, Pa Khoi Tai — so
    you can see what is upstream of you before it arrives.

    Right now: 1.37 m of a stated 3.70 m, falling 0.12 m in six hours.

    ONE NUMBER ON THAT PAGE IS COMPUTED, and I want to flag it because it is
    the only place I did arithmetic rather than quote. Everyone in Chiang Mai
    says water takes six to eight hours from Mae Tae to Nawarat. I measured
    it instead — cross-correlated the two hourly series over thirty days. The
    answer is 5 hours, correlation 0.841 across 736 compared hours. The page
    prints the correlation and the window beside it, and says plainly that it
    is a measurement of the days behind us and not a promise about the next
    flood.

    The page forecasts nothing, advises nothing, and names no crest. It hands
    that job to ปภ. and gives 1784. There is a test that holds that line.

6 · THE ENRICHMENT
-------------------
    The catalogue held 593 state health facilities — 124 hospitals and 469
    รพ.สต. — and a phone number for TEN of them.

    The clean-air register's rows are entered by each facility's own staff
    and carry phones. Matching them onto the catalogue took the count from
    10 to 141. Nothing new was crawled; the numbers were already on the disk,
    fetched for item 4.

    It refuses a match when the name belongs to two facilities, when the
    number is one of the bulk-entry defaults, or when the two pins are more
    than 5 km apart. Every number carries its source and the date.

    Also done from item 6: the 169 mapped drinking-water points, fetched in
    August and used by nothing until today.
